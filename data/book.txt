THE LIGHTHOUSE OF MERROW BAY

CHAPTER 1: THE ARRIVAL

Mira Castellan arrived at Merrow Bay on a grey Tuesday in October, carrying one suitcase and a brass compass that had belonged to her grandfather. She was twenty-six years old, and she had just been appointed the new keeper of the Merrow Bay lighthouse, a tall white tower that stood on a rocky cliff at the edge of the village.

The ferry captain, an old man named Joss Tavernor, helped her carry the suitcase up the stone steps. "Nobody has kept that light for three years," he told her. "The last keeper, Edmund Hale, left in a hurry and never came back."

Mira did not ask why. She was more interested in the tower itself. It was forty-two metres tall, with one hundred and eighty-eight steps winding up to the lamp room. At the very top sat a great glass lens, made in France in 1894, which could be seen from thirty kilometres away on a clear night.

CHAPTER 2: THE VILLAGE

Merrow Bay was a small fishing village of about three hundred people. Its main street had a bakery, a post office and a tavern called The Salted Anchor. The baker, a cheerful woman named Greta Lindqvist, gave Mira a warm loaf of bread on her first morning and said, "You will need your strength. That tower is not kind to tired legs."

The mayor of the village was Oswin Prattle, a stern man who wore the same green coat every day. He met Mira at the post office and explained her duties. She was to light the lamp every evening at sunset, clean the lens every week, and keep a daily log of ships passing the bay.

"The lamp must never go dark," said Oswin. "Not even for one night. Fishing boats depend on it."

Mira promised that it would not.

CHAPTER 3: THE LOCKED ROOM

On her fourth day, while cleaning the tower, Mira found a small wooden door on the ninth landing. It was locked. The brass key she had been given by the mayor did not fit it.

That night she asked Joss Tavernor about the door. He frowned and lowered his voice. "Edmund Hale kept his private papers in there. He was studying the old shipwreck of the Marigold, a cargo ship that sank near the bay in 1931. Some say he found something he should not have found."

The Marigold had carried silver coins, forty crates of them, according to the old records. No one had ever recovered the cargo. Mira thought about the locked door all night and could not sleep.

CHAPTER 4: THE STORM

In the second week of November, a terrible storm came from the north. The wind howled at ninety kilometres an hour, and waves crashed against the cliff. At sunset Mira climbed the one hundred and eighty-eight steps and lit the lamp, as always.

Around midnight the lamp flickered and died. The storm had damaged the oil feed. Mira remembered the mayor's words, the lamp must never go dark, and she worked for two hours in the freezing wind, repairing the pipe with her grandfather's compass case and a strip of leather from her boot.

At two in the morning the light returned. Soon after, three fishing boats from the village, The Pelican, The Dawn Runner and The Little Wren, came safely into the harbour, guided by the beam. Greta Lindqvist stood on the dock and wept with relief.

CHAPTER 5: THE SECRET

After the storm, Oswin Prattle brought Mira a second key. "Edmund left this with me," he admitted. "I was afraid of what was inside. But you saved the boats. You have earned the right."

Mira opened the locked door on the ninth landing. Inside she found a desk covered in maps and a leather notebook. Edmund Hale had worked out that the Marigold had not sunk near the bay at all. The old records were wrong. The ship lay two kilometres further north, in shallow water beside a reef called the Gull's Teeth.

At the end of the notebook, Edmund had written one last line: "The silver belongs to the village, not to any one person. Whoever finds it must share it."

In the spring, Mira, Joss and a team of divers recovered thirty-one crates of silver coins. Nine crates were lost to the sea. The village used the money to build a new school and to repair the harbour wall. Mira stayed on as keeper of the lighthouse for the rest of her life, and the lamp never went dark again.
